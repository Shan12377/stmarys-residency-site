NICU: Pharmacist Core

Pharmacist’s job in the NICU: make sure the dose is right for this baby’s weight and age, the concentration and volume are safe, the excipients are safe, and the interval fits the baby’s kidneys. Neonatal doses are tiny, so a decimal error is a 10 fold overdose.

Sources: AHA/AAP Neonatal Resuscitation Guidelines (2025); NRP Textbook 8th ed.; AAP Red Book (current edition); AAP Clinical Practice Guideline on hyperbilirubinemia (2022); ASHP/IDSA/PIDS/SIDP vancomycin consensus guideline (2020, includes pediatrics); ISMP pediatric safety guidance. Dosing references: Neofax or Lexicomp Pediatric and Neonatal (use what St. Mary’s uses).

1. The vocabulary you must use correctly

  • GA (gestational age): weeks in utero at birth.
  • PNA (postnatal age): days since birth.
  • PMA (postmenstrual age): GA + PNA in weeks. Many neonatal dosing intervals are based on PMA and PNA.
  • Birth weight vs dosing weight: know which one the order uses. Babies often drop weight the first week.
  • Term: 37 weeks or more. Preterm: under 37. Extremely preterm: under 28.

2. Safety rules that prevent the famous errors

  • Weight in kg, every time. Check the weight is current and plausible for age.
  • Check mg/kg/dose vs mg/kg/day. This is the most common pediatric error type.
  • 10 fold errors: trailing zeros (never “1.0 mg”), leading zeros (always “0.1 mg”). Double check any dose that is a round number for a tiny baby.
  • Heparin: neonatal line flushes and infusions use very low concentrations. Wrong strength heparin vials have caused infant deaths (ISMP has documented cases). Verify the concentration on every heparin order.
  • Excipients: benzyl alcohol (gasping syndrome), propylene glycol, polysorbate, ethanol. Choose preservative free products for neonates.
  • Volume: a 600 g baby cannot take adult flush volumes. Count flush and dilution volume in the fluid total.
  • Independent double check for high alert drugs and all neonatal TPN.

3. Delivery room and resuscitation (AHA/AAP 2025; NRP)

  • Ventilation is the priority. 2025 update: ventilation rate 30 to 60 per minute; deferred cord clamping for 60 seconds or longer in vigorous infants.
  • Epinephrine: use 0.1 mg/mL (1 mg/10 mL). IV or UVC: 0.02 mg/kg (range 0.01 to 0.03 mg/kg) every 3 to 5 minutes. Endotracheal: 0.1 mg/kg (range 0.05 to 0.1). The IV route is preferred ✅✅ LHSC NICU: epinephrine; Vali et al. on NRP epinephrine ranges (PMC) (ILCOR ranges: IV 0.01 to 0.03, ETT 0.05 to 0.1 mg/kg).
  • Volume expansion: normal saline 10 mL/kg over 5 to 10 minutes for suspected hypovolemia.
  • Pharmacist role: have the neonatal code doses calculated by weight before delivery for high risk births, if St. Mary’s uses a pre calculated sheet.

4. Routine newborn medications

  • Vitamin K (phytonadione): 0.5 to 1 mg IM once, to prevent vitamin K deficiency bleeding (AAP). Parents who refuse: document and educate.
  • Erythromycin 0.5% ophthalmic ointment: once, both eyes, for gonococcal ophthalmia prophylaxis.
  • Hepatitis B vaccine: within 24 hours; plus HBIG within 12 hours if the mother is HBsAg positive.

5. Neonatal sepsis (AAP Red Book; AAP clinical reports 2018 and 2019)

  • Early onset (first 72 hours): ampicillin + gentamicin.
  • Late onset: usually vancomycin (or nafcillin or oxacillin) + gentamicin, or a cephalosporin per unit protocol.
  • Meningitis concern: ampicillin + a third or fourth generation cephalosporin such as cefepime or ceftazidime (cefotaxime is no longer available in the US) [VERIFY St. Mary’s choice].
  • Ceftriaxone: avoid in neonates with hyperbilirubinemia (displaces bilirubin), and never give with IV calcium containing solutions in neonates (fatal precipitates; FDA).
  • HSV: acyclovir 20 mg/kg IV every 8 hours (adjust for kidney function); watch for nephrotoxicity and neutropenia.

Why these neonatal rules exist in the body (plain English):

  • Why early onset sepsis uses ampicillin + gentamicin: A baby infected in the first 72 hours caught the bacteria from the birth canal, mainly Group B Strep, E. coli, and Listeria. Ampicillin covers Group B Strep and Listeria (cephalosporins never cover Listeria), while gentamicin covers E. coli and teams up with ampicillin for faster killing (synergy) (AAP Red Book) ✅.
  • Two reasons ceftriaxone is avoided in neonates: First, ceftriaxone binds to calcium in the blood or IV line to form solid chalky crystals that plug up a newborn’s tiny lung and kidney vessels (fatal precipitates). Second, ceftriaxone knocks bilirubin off albumin in the blood; in a newborn with an immature blood brain barrier, free bilirubin crosses into the brain and causes permanent brain damage (kernicterus) (FDA; AAP 2022) ✅✅.
  • Why benzyl alcohol causes gasping syndrome: Adult multi dose vials (like bacteriostatic saline or multi dose enoxaparin/heparin) use benzyl alcohol as a preservative. An adult liver breaks benzyl alcohol down easily, but a newborn’s immature liver cannot clear it, so toxic benzoic acid piles up in the blood and causes severe metabolic acidosis, gasping respirations, and cardiovascular collapse ✅.

6. Gentamicin and vancomycin in neonates

  • Gentamicin: extended interval dosing; dose and interval depend on GA or PMA and PNA. Example only: a term neonate is often 4 mg/kg every 24 hours; preterm babies get longer intervals (every 36 to 48 hours). Use the unit’s chart (Neofax or Lexicomp). Draw levels per protocol.
  • Vancomycin: the 2020 consensus guideline recommends AUC guided dosing in pediatrics and neonates where possible; know whether St. Mary’s uses AUC (Bayesian software) or troughs for neonates.

8. Neonatal TPN (with Module 10)

  • Calcium and phosphate solubility is the biggest compounding risk; neonates need high amounts of both. Use the solubility curve and add phosphate first, calcium last.
  • Cysteine improves calcium and phosphate solubility and is commonly added for neonates (cysteine lowers the pH of the TPN bag slightly; in a slightly more acidic bag, calcium and phosphate stay dissolved instead of turning into solid glass like crystals).
  • Lipid dose limits and monitoring triglycerides; know whether the unit uses SMOFlipid or Intralipid.
  • Every neonatal TPN gets an independent double check.

9. Hyperbilirubinemia (AAP CPG 2022)

Pharmacist angle: drugs that displace bilirubin (ceftriaxone, sulfonamides) are avoided in jaundiced neonates.

Verify locally at St. Mary’s

  • Neonatal dosing reference used (Neofax or Lexicomp) and the gentamicin interval chart.
  • Standard concentrations for neonatal drips (dopamine, epinephrine, morphine, fentanyl, insulin, heparin).
  • NICU level (St. Mary’s lists a Level III NICU) and who verifies orders on nights.
  • Empiric sepsis and meningitis regimens.
  • Pre calculated neonatal code sheet: who makes it.

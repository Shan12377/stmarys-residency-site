📋 Needs Medication Reconciliation TIP Workflow

Transitions of Care

Post-discharge reconciliation protocol to prevent medication discrepancies and hospital readmissions:

1. Pre-Admission vs. Discharge Comparison

  • Identify all omitted pre-admission chronic medications (Statins, ACEi/ARBs, Thyroid).
  • Detect unintentional duplications (e.g. inpatient generic + outpatient brand).
  • Flag short-term inpatient medications mistakenly continued (PPIs, steroids, antibiotics).

2. High-Risk Discharge Discrepancies

  • Anticoagulation changes (Heparin/Lovenox bridge to DOAC vs Warfarin).
  • Insulin dose adjustments following inpatient hyperglycemia.
  • Heart Failure GDMT dose titration vs acute renal changes.

🧠 POLY-ACH PQA Anticholinergic Guide

Beers Safety

High-risk classes contributing to Anticholinergic Polypharmacy in seniors (≥65):

  • 1st Gen Antihistamines: Diphenhydramine, Hydroxyzine, Meclizine (Switch to Cetirizine / Loratadine)
  • TCAs: Amitriptyline, Nortriptyline (Switch to SSRI / SNRI)
  • Bladder Antispasmodics: Oxybutynin, Tolterodine (Switch to Mirabegron)
  • GI Antispasmodics: Dicyclomine, Hyoscyamine (Switch to Fiber / Polyethylene Glycol)
  • Antiemetics: Promethazine (Switch to Ondansetron)

🗣️ Natural Conversational Med Review Flow & Time-Limited / Titration Matrix

100% Compliant & Empathic

How to effortlessly transition between Active Meds, Inactive Meds (Discontinued vs Non-Adherence), and Supplements while meeting all Aspen/CMS open-ended requirements without sounding scripted.

🎙️ The Seamless Setup & Bridge Script:

"Alright [Patient Name], let's take a stroll through your medicine cabinet together. I'll read off the medication name, strength, and doctor we have on file, and I'd love for you to tell me in your own words how you usually take it at home and why your doctor prescribed it. That way, we can make sure everything is working safely and smoothly together. How does that sound?"

🟢 1. Active Medication Flow

RPh: "First up, I have your [Drug Name] [Strength] [Form] from Dr. [Doctor]. Tell me exactly how you usually take this one each day."

Patient: "I take one in the morning with coffee."

RPh (Indication Check): "Perfect. And what did your doctor share with you about what this pill does for your body?"

If Patient Unsure: "That's completely fine! Has your doctor mentioned this is for [Option 1, e.g. blood pressure] or [Option 2, e.g. heart protection]?"

🟡 2. Inactive Med (The Detective)

RPh: "I also see [Drug Name] [Strength] listed in your history, but it shows as stopped. What's the latest story on this one—did your doctor tell you to stop, or did you decide to take a break on your own?"

Scenario 1 (Doctor Stopped It):
"Got it! Did your doctor replace it with something else, or did the condition clear up?" → Document 'Discontinued by Prescriber'.

Scenario 2 (Side Effects / Cost / Refills):
"I see! Was it causing stomach upset, dizziness, or was the copay too high?" → Move to Active, Counsel, Add MTP!

🔵 3. OTCs & Vitamins Check

RPh (Before Hitting NEXT): "Before we wrap up your list, what over-the-counter vitamins, pain relievers, herbal supplements, or eye drops are you taking that we didn't mention yet?"

Documenting Clinical Sigs (ALA Rule):
• Patient says "for my heart" (CoQ10/Fish Oil) → 'cardiovascular health'.
• Patient says "for my bones" (Vitamin D/Calcium) → 'bone health'.
• Follow ALA Rule: Quantity 1, no mg in sig text!

🙅 Master Objection Rebuttals & Strategic Framings

Call Strategy
1. "I already do this with my doctor."
"I love that you have a great relationship with your doctor—that is the foundation of your health! Think of your doctor as the architect and me as the structural engineer. While your doctor focuses on the diagnosis, I look exclusively at the chemistry across all seven of your prescriptions, vitamins, and supplements to make sure no two medications are fighting each other."
2. "I don't have time right now."
"I completely respect your time. Taking 15 minutes right now saves you hours of doctor visits or side effects down the road. If we find even one medication you no longer need or a way to simplify your dosing, I've just given you hours back every month. Shall we dive in?"
3. "I've been taking these for years; I'm fine."
"I'm glad you've been stable, but our bodies change every year. What worked three years ago might be putting a strain on your kidneys or liver today. My job is to make sure your regimen is updated for who you are today, not who you were years ago."
4. "Is this going to cost me anything?"
"Not a single penny. This is a complimentary clinical benefit fully covered by your health plan to keep you healthy and out of the hospital. In fact, part of my review is looking for ways to save you money on monthly copays."
5. "I don't like giving info over the phone."
"I appreciate your caution! I am Dr. Shallanda Hunter, a licensed clinical pharmacist. I already have your medication list from your insurance claims; I'm not calling to ask for your data, I'm calling to make sure how you take them at home matches what's on file."
6. "I just did this 2 weeks ago." (The Safety Sync)
"I completely understand! The reason I'm back on the line is that your health plan noticed a recent update in your profile. Think of me like a security guard for your medicine cabinet. If a new pill enters, I do a 5-minute 'Safety Sync' to make sure it won't interfere with your other meds."

📚 Clinical Disease-State Education Master

Education Reference

Teaching Options (Teach at least 1):

  • Goal of Medication: Lowers blood pressure to protect heart and kidneys, reducing risk of stroke and heart attack.
  • Monitoring Frequency: Check BP once daily at the same time, or whenever feeling dizzy/unwell.
  • Lifestyle: DASH diet (rich in fruits/veggies/potassium), reduce sodium intake (<2,300 mg/day), 150 mins exercise/week.

Reframing Patient Denial ("I don't have high BP"):

"Even when your blood pressure feels completely normal, this medication works best when taken every single day to protect your heart and blood vessels."

The Rule of 15 (Blood Sugar < 70 mg/dL):

  1. Step 1 (15g Fast Carbs): Drink 4 oz (1/2 cup) fruit juice/soda, 3-4 glucose tablets, or 1 tbsp honey. Avoid chocolate/fats initially.
  2. Step 2 (Wait 15 Mins): Rest 15 minutes and re-check blood sugar.
  3. Step 3 (Repeat if Needed): If still under 70 mg/dL, take another 15g carbs and wait 15 mins.
  4. Step 4 (Stabilize): Once back above 70, eat a snack with protein (cheese + crackers or peanut butter).

Beta-Blocker Warning: Metoprolol/Carvedilol can mask shakiness and racing heart during hypoglycemia. Sweating is often the only remaining symptom.

HFrEF guideline-directed medical therapy reduces mortality by >60% when all 4 pillars are optimized:

Pillar 1: ARNI / ACEi / ARB
Sacubitril/Valsartan (Entresto) preferred. Reverses cardiac remodeling.

Carvedilol, Metoprolol Succinate ER, or Bisoprolol ONLY. Slows heart rate and reduces sudden death.
Pillar 3: MRA
Spironolactone, Eplerenone, or Finerenone (Kerendia). Reduces myocardial fibrosis.
Pillar 4: SGLT2 Inhibitor
Empagliflozin (Jardiance) or Dapagliflozin (Farxiga). Cardiorenal protection and reduces hospitalizations.

📐 The ALA Sig Rule & Documentation Standards

QA Rule

The ALA Split Dose / Multiple Tablets Rule:

If a patient takes 1,200 mg daily from 600 mg tablets (2 tablets):
Set Quantity to 1. Sig: "Take 1 tablet by mouth twice daily" or "Take 1 tablet by mouth once daily".
NEVER write "Take 2 tablets". NEVER put milligrams in the sig field. The system calculates the total based on single dose units.

Standard Sig Translation Dictionary

What Patient SaysCorrect Outcomes Sig Entry
"I take one in the morning."Take 1 tablet by mouth once daily in the morning.
"I take two pills every morning."Take 1 tablet by mouth once daily in the morning. (Quantity: 1 per dose)
"Half a pill for pressure."Take 1/2 tablet by mouth once daily for high blood pressure.
"I inject it every Sunday."Inject subcutaneously once weekly.
"Drops in both eyes at night."Instill 1 drop in each eye once daily at bedtime.
"Puff of my rescue inhaler when tight."Inhale 1-2 puffs by mouth every 4-6 hours as needed for shortness of breath.

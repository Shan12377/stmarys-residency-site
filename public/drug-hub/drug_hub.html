The Golden Rule of Hospital Pharmacy: Hospital Protocol Beats the Textbook

Before you quote Lexicomp or UpToDate to a physician or nurse, always check St. Mary’s Cerner Order Set, Hospital Policy, or Smart Pump Library first (see Stay_Current).

  • Why: If Lexicomp lists three ways to dose a heparin drip, DKA insulin drip, magnesium sulfate, or vancomycin AUC, you must verify against the exact nomogram and standard bag concentration built into St. Mary’s smart pumps. Using an outside concentration that does not match the pump library creates a high risk medication error (ISMP High Alert Medications 2024).

Side by side guide: Trissel’s vs Lexicomp vs NeoFax vs Sanford vs Briggs

ResourceWhen to open it firstWhat trap to watch out for (Plain English)
Trissel’s IV Compatibility (accessed via the IV Compatibility tab in Lexicomp or Micromedex)Nurse asks: “Can I Y-site vancomycin with piperacillin tazobactam?” or “How long is this diluted bag stable?”Check the right tab and concentration:
• Y-Site tab: Two separate bags meeting at the tubing port near the patient (only mixes for a few minutes in the line).
• Admixture (Solution) tab: Mixed together inside the same IV bag for hours.
• Concentration matters: Ampicillin is stable much longer in NS than D5W, and high concentrations precipitate faster. Vancomycin and beta lactams can precipitate at certain concentrations.
• “Variable” or “No Data”: Click into the study details. If your patient’s concentration is higher than the tested concentration, or if there is no data, treat as Incompatible and flush the line with NS between drugs or use a separate lumen (see IV_Compatibility).
Lexicomp (Adult) vs MicromedexEvery routine adult verification: renal CrCl adjustments, hepatic adjustments, black box warnings, max daily doses, administration rates, and drug interactions.How to use them together:
• Lexicomp is fastest at the bench for clean dosing by indication, CrCl cutoffs, and administration rate (push vs piggyback).
• Micromedex is your tie breaker when Lexicomp is vague, when you need deeper toxicology (Poisindex), or when checking detailed Trissel’s solution stability tables.
NeoFax vs Lexi-Peds (Taketomo Pediatric Handbook)NeoFax: Any baby in the NICU or under 28 days old.
Lexi-Peds: Infants over 28 days, children, and adolescents in PICU or general pediatrics.		Never use Adult Lexicomp for a baby:
• Premature babies clear drugs slowly because their kidneys and liver are still developing.
• NeoFax doses by Gestational Age (GA) or Postmenstrual Age (PMA = GA + weeks since birth) plus Postnatal Age (PNA = days since birth) (for example, NICU gentamicin every 24, 36, or 48 hours; see NICU_Core).
• Lexi-Peds gives weight based mg/kg dosing and the adult ceiling cap (never exceed the adult dose in a heavy teenager; see Pediatric_Neonatal_Dosing).
St. Mary’s Antibiogram + Sanford GuideDoctor asks: “What empiric antibiotic should we start for this ICU pneumonia or urosepsis?” or “Does oral step down work here?”Local resistance beats national averages:
• Sanford Guide tells you which bugs a drug covers in general and gives clean renal antibiotic dosing tables.
• St. Mary’s Antibiogram tells you what percentage of local Pseudomonas, E. coli, or MRSA at St. Mary’s is actually susceptible this year (see ID_Core and Antimicrobial_Guide).
Briggs (Pregnancy) & LactMed (Breastfeeding)OB nurse or physician asks: “Can this mom take labetalol, enoxaparin, or sertraline while pregnant or breastfeeding?”Letters A, B, C, D, X are gone:
• The FDA replaced pregnancy letter categories with the PLLR narrative (Risk Summary, Clinical Considerations, Data). Use Briggs or the FDA label PLLR section for pregnancy.
• For breastfeeding, open LactMed (NIH, free) and check the Relative Infant Dose (RID): an RID below 10% is generally considered compatible with breastfeeding for full term infants (see Pregnancy_Lactation).
CredibleMeds + ISMP ListsChecking QT prolongation stacking (ondansetron + haloperidol + azithromycin + methadone) or whether a pill can be crushed for an NG/OG tube.• CredibleMeds separates drugs that clearly cause Torsades de Pointes (“Known Risk”) from drugs that only prolong QTc under certain conditions (check K above 4.0 and Mg above 2.0).
• ISMP Do Not Crush (and Lexicomp’s Administration section) stops you from crushing extended release (ER/XR), enteric coated (EC), or hazardous tablets (see Med_Safety_and_Florida_Law).
